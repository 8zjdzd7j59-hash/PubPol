**Congress Is Broken, but the Constitution Isn't Beyond Repair**

[Your Name]

[Your Section]

---

Most Americans learn early on that the Constitution has lasted longer than almost any other written constitution in the world, and that fact alone makes it easy to assume the system works the way it was supposed to. I do not think that assumption holds up once you look at how the government actually operates today. The real question behind this assignment is not whether the Constitution still exists on paper. It is whether the government it created can pass laws people want, represent people fairly, and respond when something goes wrong. In my opinion, the answer is mixed. Michael Kammen points out that the Constitution is short and durable, but also full of compromises and ambiguities that people have been fighting over since 1787 (Kammen 46-47). That pattern of "conflict-within-consensus" helps explain why the system can keep running without ever feeling fully democratic (Kammen 51). The legislative branch was supposed to be the most representative part of the whole setup, yet right now it is the branch that struggles the most. That is why I believe major reform is needed, even though I am not arguing we should throw out the Constitution and start over. Smaller, targeted changes to how we elect leaders and how much power each branch holds would go a long way toward fixing what is broken without giving up the separation of powers the Framers cared about.

Before getting into the branches, I want to lay out how I am using a couple of key terms and where this essay is headed. A functional government, as I use the term here, means a government that can spot problems, debate solutions, pass laws, and carry them out while still being accountable to voters. A democratic government means something more specific: citizens who are supposed to be political equals actually have a real chance to shape what the government does, and the people in power can be thrown out if they abuse that power (Dahl 37). The Framers never tried to build a direct democracy. They built a republic where the people would rule indirectly through elected representatives, state governments, and a divided federal government. Madison wrote in Federalist No. 10 that a large republic could keep factions from taking over by spreading interests out and forcing majorities to form coalitions, and in Federalist No. 51 he argued that each branch had to have its own ambitions so no single branch could run everything (Hamilton, Madison, and Jay 46-51, 319-20). Those ideas still matter, but the Constitution also included compromises like the Senate and slavery that gave some groups more say than others from the very beginning (Kammen 49-50). After explaining how each branch was designed and how it behaves now, I will look at who wins and who loses under the current structure, and then make the case for reform.

Article I puts lawmaking power in a bicameral Congress, Article II creates an indirectly elected president with veto and appointment powers, and Article III establishes life-tenured federal judges insulated from elections. On paper, Congress represents the people, the president runs the government, and the courts protect rights. In practice, Congress is where the gap between design and reality shows up most clearly. David Mayhew argues that members of Congress are motivated above all by reelection, and that most of what they do, advertising, credit claiming, and position taking, is aimed at keeping their jobs rather than making good national policy (Mayhew 156). The structure of Congress actually helps with that goal. Members get their own offices, staff, franking privileges, and committee assignments that let them bring home particular benefits to their districts without stepping on each other's toes (Mayhew 156-57). Parties mostly leave members alone to vote their constituencies, which sounds democratic on the surface but also means there is little pressure to build stable majorities for big national legislation (Mayhew 158). Add partisan polarization, the sixty-vote threshold in the Senate, and the fact that every state gets two senators regardless of population, and popular ideas often die before they reach the president's desk (Levinson 17-22). Congress was designed to be the first branch, but Mayhew's account suggests it works best as a collection of individual political entrepreneurs, not as an institution that can solve national problems on its own.

While Congress stalls, the presidency looks more powerful than it was probably meant to be, but Charles Jones warns against treating the president as the whole government. The United States has a separated system where responsibility is spread out and no one actor controls everything (Jones 200). Presidents often have to let legislators work out the details of major bills, and divided government is a normal outcome when elections are held separately for different offices (Jones 201, 203-204). That helps explain why modern presidents lean on executive orders when Congress will not move.

Many Americans also assume the Supreme Court can fix problems when the elected branches fail. Gerald Rosenberg argues the opposite. Courts depend on political support to enforce their rulings, and even *Brown v. Board of Education* did little until Congress acted a decade later (Rosenberg 276-277). Rosenberg compares a court's contribution to cutting a ribbon on a finished project, not building it (Rosenberg 277). The least democratic branch ends up deciding some of the biggest questions about rights, even though it may be the least equipped to carry those decisions through.

These problems do not hit every group the same way. Rogers Smith argues that American political culture combines liberal beliefs in equality with "ascriptive Americanism," the idea that some groups naturally deserve more status than others (Smith 5-6). For most of U.S. history, laws excluded most people from full citizenship based on race, nationality, or gender, and even after Reconstruction, poll taxes and literacy tests locked Black voters out while looking neutral on paper (Smith 11-12). That history still echoes in restrictive voting laws, Senate malapportionment that underweights residents of large states, and court victories for women and LGBTQ Americans when Congress would not act. Mancur Olson adds another layer. When a benefit is shared by a huge group, any one person's share of the gain is tiny, so people often sit back and assume someone else will fight for the policy they want (Olson 405-406). Narrow, well-organized interests lobby Congress more easily than broad public interest groups (Olson 409). Smith and Olson both show why the rules of the game push power toward some communities and away from others.

The Constitution still has real strengths. Kammen notes that its compromises and ambiguities have produced decades of "conflict-within-consensus" without collapse (Kammen 51), and Jones reminds us that diffused responsibility can force bargaining that no single party would accept alone (Jones 203). I take those strengths seriously.

Still, not collapsing is a pretty low standard for a country that calls itself a democracy. When the Senate can kill bills most Americans support, when presidents rewrite policy through executive action because Congress will not govern, and when courts announce rights that may never be enforced without legislation, it is fair to ask whether the government works better for some people than for everyone else. Congress was built to be the place where democratic debate happens, but Mayhew's account suggests it is organized around reelection rather than national problem solving. The presidency moves faster inside Jones's separated system, and the courts attract public attention even though Rosenberg shows their power is more limited than most people think. Fixing that imbalance does not require abolishing the presidency or packing the courts. It requires giving Congress the ability to do its job again and cleaning up the parts of the structure that skew representation.

That is where I land on reform. I do not think swapping out the entire Constitution for a parliamentary system is realistic, and Kammen's history suggests Americans treat the federal Constitution as almost permanent in a way they do not treat state constitutions (Kammen 51-52). What does seem possible is a package of smaller fixes: changing or eliminating the filibuster so a majority in the Senate can actually govern, reforming the Electoral College so the presidential winner reflects the national vote, passing stronger voting rights laws so states cannot use race-neutral rules to exclude voters the way Smith describes, and writing clearer statutes so presidents cannot fill in whatever details they want. None of these changes would be easy. Olson's logic of collective action suggests organized minorities will fight hard to protect advantages they already have (Olson 411-412). But taken together, these reforms target the specific problems I have been describing without tearing down the whole Madisonian framework.

The government the Constitution created can still function, but it is not democratic enough by any reasonable standard, and Congress is the branch that illustrates the problem most clearly. The Framers put the House and Senate at the center of the republic because they trusted elected representatives to turn popular will into law, but a legislature built around reelection incentives struggles to do that work today. The presidency and judiciary have filled part of the vacuum, which keeps the country moving but concentrates power in places where ordinary voters have less direct say. Marginalized communities pay the highest price, whether through the legacy of ascriptive hierarchy Smith describes, underrepresentation in the Senate, or the free-rider problems Olson identifies among large groups that lack organized voice. I believe targeted reform can address those failures. The Constitution does not need to be scrapped. It needs to be updated so that the government works for the people it claims to represent, not just for the groups the original rules favored.
