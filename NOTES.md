**Congress Is Broken, but the Constitution Isn't Beyond Repair**

[Your Name]

[Your Section]

---

Most Americans learn early on that the Constitution has lasted longer than almost any other written constitution in the world, and that fact alone makes it easy to assume the system works the way it was supposed to. I do not think that assumption holds up once you look at how the government actually operates in 2026. The real question behind this assignment is not whether the Constitution still exists on paper. It is whether the government it created can pass laws people want, represent people fairly, and respond when something goes wrong. In my opinion, the answer is mixed. The system can still function during normal periods, but it has serious democratic problems built into its structure, and Congress has fallen behind the presidency and the courts when it comes to getting things done. The legislative branch was supposed to be the most democratic part of the whole setup, yet right now it is the branch that struggles the most. That is why I believe major reform is needed, even though I am not arguing we should throw out the Constitution and start over. Smaller, targeted changes to how we elect leaders and how much power each branch holds would go a long way toward fixing what is broken without giving up the checks and balances Madison and the other Framers cared about.

Before getting into the branches, I want to lay out how I am using a couple of key terms and where this essay is headed. A functional government, as I use the term here, means a government that can spot problems, debate solutions, pass laws, and carry them out while still being accountable to voters. A democratic government means something more specific: citizens who are supposed to be political equals actually have a real chance to shape what the government does, and the people in power can be thrown out if they abuse that power (Dahl 37). The Framers never tried to build a direct democracy. They built a republic where the people would rule indirectly through elected representatives, state governments, and a divided federal government. Madison wrote in Federalist No. 10 that a large republic could keep factions from taking over by spreading interests out and forcing majorities to form coalitions, and in Federalist No. 51 he argued that each branch had to have its own ambitions so no single branch could run everything (Hamilton, Madison, and Jay 46-51, 319-20). Those ideas still matter, but the Constitution also included compromises like the Senate and the Electoral College that gave some groups more say than others from the very beginning. After explaining how each branch was designed, I will look at who wins and who loses under the current structure, and then make the case for reform.

Congress got the most attention in Philadelphia. Article I puts lawmaking power in the House and Senate, with different election cycles and different constituencies so neither chamber could rush through policy without thinking it through. The president under Article II was meant to execute the laws, lead the military, and face voters every four years, but the Electoral College stood between the people and the office in a way the House did not. Article III created the federal courts, and federal judges serve for life, which keeps them away from election-year pressure but also means average citizens cannot vote them out when they disagree with a ruling. On paper, the three branches fit together neatly. Congress represents the people, the president runs the government, and the courts protect rights. That was the theory.

What happens in practice looks quite different. Congress is stuck. Partisan fighting, the sixty-vote threshold in the Senate, and the way committee chairs control which bills even get a vote mean that popular ideas often die before they reach the president's desk (Mann and Ornstein 112-18). The filibuster is not in the Constitution, but senators treat it like a permanent rule anyway, and a minority can block legislation even when most of the country wants it passed. The Senate makes this worse because every state gets two senators regardless of population, so a voter in Wyoming has far more influence over the chamber than a voter in Texas (Levinson 17-22). While Congress stalls, the presidency has picked up the slack. Presidents use executive orders, emergency powers, and vague laws that punt hard decisions to federal agencies. Howell and Lewis found that modern presidents regularly act on their own when Congress will not cooperate, which can get policy moving but also lets the White House operate without the public debate that ought to happen in the legislature (Howell and Lewis 4-9). The courts have moved into the gap as well. The Constitution never spells out judicial review in plain language, but Marshall's decision in *Marbury v. Madison* gave the Supreme Court the power to strike down laws it considers unconstitutional, and today the Court weighs in on voting rights, gerrymandering, money in politics, and more. So the branch with the weakest democratic connection to voters has ended up deciding some of the most important questions about who counts as a full participant in American politics.

These problems do not hit every group the same way, and that matters for judging whether the system is democratic enough. Black voters have dealt with voter ID laws, long lines at polling places, felony disenfranchisement, and redrawn districts that water down their influence, and Anderson makes a strong case that fighting over the ballot never really ended after the Voting Rights Act (Anderson 3-8). Poorer Americans vote less often and rely on programs like Medicaid, housing aid, and workplace protections that need federal legislation to work consistently. When Congress cannot pass anything, those people are left waiting while states that refuse to expand benefits leave them without coverage. If you live in a big state like California or New York, your vote counts less in the Senate simply because of where you live, and the Electoral College has put presidents in office who lost the national popular vote more than once. Women and LGBTQ Americans have won key rights through court decisions when Congress would not act, which shows the courts can step in but also puts basic equality in the hands of nine unelected justices. Native tribes occupy an even stranger spot in the system. They are recognized as sovereign in some areas but still fall under broad federal authority, so a new administration can shift policy on land, health care, and criminal jurisdiction without Congress passing a single new law. None of this means the Constitution was designed to hurt these groups on purpose, but the rules of the game push political power toward some communities and away from others.

I would be leaving out half the story if I did not acknowledge what the Constitution still does well. Dividing power among three branches has kept any one person from seizing total control, and letting states experiment with policy has produced real innovation in areas like education and health care. Ginsburg and Huq warn that democracies usually fall apart slowly, through presidents grabbing more power and politicians ignoring norms, rather than in one dramatic collapse (Ginsburg and Huq 41-45). Elections still matter, courts can still push back against illegal actions, and Congress can still investigate the executive branch when members choose to use that authority. Those tools help explain why the country has not fallen into dictatorship, and I take that seriously.

Still, not collapsing is a pretty low standard for a country that calls itself a democracy. When the Senate can kill bills most Americans support, when presidents rewrite policy with a pen because Congress will not show up to work, and when courts draw the lines around who gets to vote, it is fair to ask whether the government works better for some people than for everyone else. Congress was built to be the place where democratic debate happens, but it has become the bottleneck. The presidency moves faster, and the courts attract more attention every term, but neither was meant to replace the legislature as the main source of lawmaking. Fixing that imbalance does not require abolishing the presidency or packing the courts. It requires giving Congress the ability to do its job again and cleaning up the parts of the structure that skew representation.

That is where I land on reform. I do not think swapping out the entire Constitution for a parliamentary system is realistic, and trying to do so would create chaos that could hurt the same minority groups who already depend on constitutional protections. What does seem possible is a package of smaller fixes: changing or eliminating the filibuster so a majority in the Senate can actually govern, reforming the Electoral College so the presidential winner reflects the national vote, passing stronger voting rights laws so states cannot chip away at access, and writing clearer statutes so presidents cannot fill in whatever details they want. None of these changes would be easy. Every one of them would face fierce opposition. But taken together, they target the specific problems I have been describing without tearing down the whole Madisonian framework.

The government the Constitution created can still function, but it is not democratic enough by any reasonable standard, and Congress is the branch that illustrates the problem most clearly. The Framers put the House and Senate at the center of the republic because they trusted elected representatives to turn popular will into law, but polarization and procedural roadblocks have made that harder than it has been in generations. The presidency and judiciary have filled the vacuum, which keeps the country moving but concentrates power in places where ordinary voters have less direct say. Marginalized communities pay the highest price, whether through underrepresentation in the Senate, barriers at the ballot box, or reliance on courts and presidents when Congress fails to legislate. I believe targeted reform can address those failures. The Constitution does not need to be scrapped. It needs to be updated so that the government works for the people it claims to represent, not just for the groups the original rules favored.
