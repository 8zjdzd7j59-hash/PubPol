**Why Congress Fails: The First Branch and the Case for Legislative Reform**

[Your Name]

[Your Section]

---

When Congress shut down over a budget fight a few years back, a lot of my friends blamed the president, which makes sense on the surface since the White House dominates the news, but shutdowns happen because Congress cannot pass spending bills, not because the president unilaterally closes the government. That confusion points to a larger question this paper takes up: whether the government the Constitution created is sufficiently functional and democratic, narrowed here to the branch the Framers trusted with lawmaking first. A functional legislature can identify problems, debate solutions, pass laws, and stay accountable to voters; a democratic legislature gives political equals a real chance to shape policy and holds elected officials answerable for their choices. This essay will explain what Article I was designed to do, use David Mayhew's electoral connection theory to show why Congress behaves as it does today, examine how congressional gridlock shifts power to the presidency and courts, analyze how different groups are affected unevenly, and argue for targeted legislative reform rather than a constitutional overhaul. Congress has become the weakest link in the constitutional system because its electoral incentives, Senate structure, and procedural rules prevent it from turning popular will into law, and major reform focused on the legislature itself is therefore necessary to make American government democratic enough to work.

The Framers did not design Congress as a direct democracy, but they did design it as the branch most connected to the people. Michael Kammen explains that Americans came to see a constitution as a "written frame of government setting fixed limits on the use of power," and in the newer view, that document became "the instrument by which the people entrusted power to their agents" (Kammen 48). Article I carries out that logic by vesting legislative power in a bicameral Congress, with the House elected every two years from local districts and the Senate giving each state equal representation. Madison argued in Federalist No. 51 that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition," so no single branch could dominate the others (Hamilton, Madison, and Jay 319-20). Kammen adds that when the founders spoke of political liberty, they meant "the freedom to be a participant, to vote and hold public office" (Kammen 48). Congress was supposed to be where that participation mattered most, turning debate among representatives into national policy. The constitutional framework therefore set a high standard: if the first branch cannot legislate, the whole system's claim to democratic legitimacy starts to crack, because lawmaking is the function the Constitution assigns to the body closest to voters.

Congress falls short of that standard today for two connected reasons, and David Mayhew's theory of the electoral connection explains the first. Mayhew argues that members of Congress are rational politicians whose main goal is reelection, and that most of what they do falls into advertising, credit claiming, and position taking rather than national problem solving (Mayhew 156). The institution is organized to help them succeed at that goal. Mayhew writes that "if a group of planners sat down and tried to design a pair of American national assemblies with the goal of serving members' electoral needs year in and year out, they would be hard pressed to improve on what exists" (Mayhew 156). Each member gets staff, franking privileges, and committee assignments that can deliver benefits to a home district. Parties in Congress "are more useful for what they are not than for what they are," because they mostly leave members free to vote locally instead of building a coherent national program (Mayhew 158). Mayhew puts it bluntly: "There is no member of either house who would not be politically injured... by being made to toe a party line on all policies" (Mayhew 158). That arrangement helps explain why Congress struggles to govern even when it functions well for individual careers. A member who spends time on a national issue that does not help the district risks losing reelection, while a member who brings home projects and takes popular positions gains credit without solving problems that affect the whole country. The second reason is structural. The Senate gives every state two senators regardless of population, and the filibuster has become a routine sixty-vote requirement even though it is not in the Constitution. Together, those rules let a minority block legislation that majorities of Americans support on issues like health care, voting rights, and immigration. Congress was built to be the place where the country argues and decides, but it has become the place where proposals go to stall.

That stall matters for the whole constitutional system because power does not disappear when Congress stops legislating; it simply moves elsewhere, which makes legislative failure look like executive or judicial success. Charles Jones reminds us that "the president is not the presidency" and that the United States has "a separated system" rather than a presidential one (Jones 200). When Congress will not act, presidents turn to executive orders and agency rules to fill the gap. Courts play a similar backup role. Gerald Rosenberg argues that a court's contribution to reform is "more like the cutting of the ribbon on a new project than its construction" (Rosenberg 277), and that even *Brown v. Board of Education* did little until Congress passed civil rights legislation years later (Rosenberg 276-277). The presidency and judiciary may therefore look more effective than Congress, but that appearance is misleading for two reasons. First, presidents and judges are less directly accountable to voters than members of Congress, so policy made through executive action or court rulings weakens the link between popular will and public law. Second, those branches were not designed to replace the legislature as the primary source of policymaking. When Congress fails, citizens get government without the democratic debate the Constitution expected, which supports the claim that the legislative branch is where the system's democratic problem is most serious.

Congressional failure also lands unevenly across American society, which shows that the problem is not just abstract gridlock but a pattern that advantages some groups over others. Rogers Smith's "multiple traditions" thesis helps explain why. He argues that American political culture combines liberal beliefs in equality with "ascriptive Americanism," a tradition in which "some groups are on top and others are below" and those on top are "deemed to be deserving of the rights and benefits the liberal tradition can offer" (Smith 5-6). Smith notes that for over eighty percent of U.S. history, laws declared most of the world's population ineligible for full citizenship because of race, nationality, or gender (Smith 5). After Reconstruction briefly expanded rights, later decades brought registration systems, poll taxes, and civics tests that "appeared race-neutral but were designed and administered to disfranchise blacks" (Smith 11-12). Congress eventually passed civil rights laws, but the Senate still underweights Black voters and residents of large diverse states like California. Poor Americans who need Medicaid, housing aid, or higher minimum wages depend on federal legislation that a gridlocked Congress often cannot pass. Mancur Olson adds another layer. When a benefit is shared by a huge number of people, "the individual in any large group with a common interest will reap only a minute share of the gains" from any lobbying effort, which produces what Olson calls the "let George do it" problem (Olson 405-406). Narrow, well-organized interests can lobby Congress effectively because each member's stake in the outcome is large, while millions of voters who would benefit from cleaner air, cheaper health care, or stronger voting protections rarely organize with the same intensity (Olson 409-411). Congress was supposed to represent the people, but when it is paralyzed or captured by organized minorities, marginalized communities pay the highest price.

Some defenders of the status quo would argue that the Constitution has endured because its ambiguities allow "conflict-within-consensus" without collapse, as Kammen describes (Kammen 51), and that Mayhew's account simply shows Congress working as designed for a separated republic. That argument has force, but it actually strengthens the case for legislative reform rather than defeating it. A legislature built to help 535 politicians keep their jobs is not the same thing as a legislature built to solve national problems, and a system that survives by shifting power to less representative institutions is functional without being democratic. Kammen also notes that Americans have treated the federal Constitution as "more nearly permanent" than their state constitutions (Kammen 51-52), which means a full constitutional rewrite is unrealistic. Targeted reforms aimed at Congress are not. Eliminating or limiting the filibuster would let Senate majorities govern. Stronger voting rights legislation would make it harder for states to use race-neutral rules to exclude voters, the kind of tactic Smith describes in the post-Reconstruction South (Smith 11-12). Clearer statutes would reduce the temptation for presidents to fill gaps Congress leaves open. Olson's logic of collective action predicts that groups benefiting from the status quo will fight hard to protect it (Olson 411-412), but those fights are worth having because they target the branch where the democratic breakdown is most obvious.

Congress has become the weakest link in the constitutional system because its electoral incentives, Senate structure, and procedural rules prevent it from turning popular will into law, and major reform focused on the legislature itself is therefore necessary to make American government democratic enough to work. Article I created a branch meant to connect citizens to national policy through elections and deliberation, yet Mayhew shows that the modern Congress is organized around reelection instead of governing, while Smith and Olson explain why marginalized communities and large unorganized publics lose out when that happens. The presidency and courts may look more active when Congress stalls, but Jones and Rosenberg suggest that activity reflects legislative failure rather than a healthier democracy. If Congress cannot legislate, Americans will continue to get policy from institutions that are harder to hold accountable, and the gap between what majorities want and what government delivers will keep widening. That matters beyond this paper because the health of a republic depends on whether ordinary citizens believe their participation actually shapes lawmaking. Restoring Congress does not require scrapping the Constitution. It requires fixing the first branch so that the government works for the people it claims to represent.
