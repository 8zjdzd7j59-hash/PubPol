**Why Congress Fails: The First Branch and the Case for Legislative Reform**

[Your Name]

[Your Section]

---

When Congress shut down over a budget fight a few years back, a lot of my friends blamed the president. That reaction makes sense on the surface, since the White House gets most of the attention on the news. But shutdowns happen because Congress cannot pass spending bills, not because the president wakes up one morning and decides to close the government. I think that confusion says something important about American politics. We treat the presidency like the whole government, even though the Constitution puts lawmaking power in Congress first. In my opinion, the legislative branch is the weakest link in the constitutional system today. Article I was built to turn popular will into law through elected representatives, but the modern Congress is better at helping members win reelection than at governing the country. Major reform is needed, and it should focus on Congress itself: the Senate's structure, the rules that let minorities block legislation, and the incentives that push members to serve narrow constituencies instead of the national interest.

This paper narrows in on Congress rather than treating all three branches equally. First, I will define what I mean by a functional and democratic legislature and explain what the Framers expected Congress to do under Article I. Second, I will use David Mayhew's theory of the electoral connection to show why the institution behaves the way it does now. Third, I will briefly compare Congress to the presidency and the courts, not to praise those branches, but to show what happens when the legislature stops working. Finally, I will argue that different groups in American society are affected unevenly by congressional failure, and that targeted legislative reform offers the most realistic path forward.

A functional legislature is one that can identify problems, debate solutions, pass laws, and remain accountable to voters. A democratic legislature adds another requirement: citizens who are supposed to be political equals need a real chance to influence what gets enacted, and the people they elect need to answer for their choices. The Framers did not design Congress as a direct democracy. Michael Kammen explains that they saw constitutions as "written frame[s] of government setting fixed limits on the use of power," and in the newer American view, the written constitution became "the instrument by which the people entrusted power to their agents" (Kammen 48). That is what Article I was supposed to do. It vests legislative power in a bicameral Congress, with the House elected every two years from local districts and the Senate originally chosen by state legislatures before the Seventeenth Amendment made senators directly elected. Madison argued in Federalist No. 51 that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition," so no branch could dominate the others (Hamilton, Madison, and Jay 319-20). Congress was the branch most tied to the people, and Kammen notes that when the founders spoke of political liberty, they meant "the freedom to be a participant, to vote and hold public office" (Kammen 48). On paper, the first branch was also the most democratic branch.

The gap between that design and today's reality is where Mayhew's argument becomes useful. Mayhew does not claim that members of Congress are evil or stupid. He says they are rational politicians whose main goal is reelection, and that most of their behavior falls into three categories: advertising, credit claiming, and position taking (Mayhew 156). What surprised me reading him is how well the institution is organized for that purpose. Mayhew writes that "if a group of planners sat down and tried to design a pair of American national assemblies with the goal of serving members' electoral needs year in and year out, they would be hard pressed to improve on what exists" (Mayhew 156). Each member gets a Capitol Hill office, staff, franking privileges, and committee assignments that can be used to bring benefits back home. Mayhew also points out that parties in Congress "are more useful for what they are not than for what they are," because they mostly leave members free to vote their districts instead of forcing them into a coherent national program (Mayhew 158). He puts it bluntly: "There is no member of either house who would not be politically injured... by being made to toe a party line on all policies" (Mayhew 158). That might sound democratic, since members are responding to local voters. But it also means Congress often cannot assemble durable majorities for national problems that do not map neatly onto any one district.

The constitutional framework makes this worse in the Senate. Every state gets two senators regardless of population, and the filibuster has become a routine sixty-vote requirement even though it is not in the Constitution. Popular bills on health care, voting rights, or immigration can die in the Senate even when most Americans want action. Congress was designed to be the place where the country argues and decides. Instead, it has become the place where proposals go to stall.

That stall forces the other branches to pick up slack. Charles Jones reminds us that "the president is not the presidency" and that the United States has "a separated system" rather than a presidential one (Jones 200). When Congress will not act, presidents turn to executive orders and agency rules. Gerald Rosenberg makes a similar point about courts. He argues that a court's contribution to reform is "more like the cutting of the ribbon on a new project than its construction" (Rosenberg 277), and that even *Brown v. Board of Education* did little until Congress passed civil rights legislation years later (Rosenberg 276-277). The presidency and judiciary may look more active, but that often reflects congressional weakness, not better democracy.

Congressional failure also lands differently on different communities, and that matters for judging whether the system is democratic enough. Rogers Smith's "multiple traditions" thesis helps explain why. He argues that American political culture has never rested on liberal beliefs alone. It has also been shaped by "ascriptive Americanism," a tradition in which "some groups are on top and others are below" and those on top are "deemed to be deserving of the rights and benefits the liberal tradition can offer" (Smith 5-6). Smith notes that for over eighty percent of U.S. history, laws declared most of the world's population ineligible for full citizenship because of race, nationality, or gender (Smith 5). After Reconstruction briefly expanded rights, later decades brought what Smith describes as registration systems, poll taxes, and civics tests that "appeared race-neutral but were designed and administered to disfranchise blacks" (Smith 11-12). Congress eventually passed civil rights laws, but the Senate's structure still underweights Black voters and other residents of large diverse states. Poor Americans who need Medicaid, housing aid, or higher minimum wages depend on legislation that a gridlocked Congress often cannot pass. Mancur Olson adds another reason broad groups lose in Congress. When a benefit is shared by a huge number of people, "the successful boycott or strike or lobbying action will bring the better price or wage for everyone in the relevant category, so the individual in any large group with a common interest will reap only a minute share of the gains" (Olson 405-406). Olson calls this the "let George do it" problem (Olson 406). Narrow, well-organized interests can lobby Congress effectively because each member's stake in the outcome is large. Millions of ordinary voters who would benefit from cleaner air, cheaper health care, or stronger voting protections rarely organize with the same intensity (Olson 409-411). Congress was supposed to represent the people. In practice, it often responds best to the groups that are easiest to organize.

Kammen argues that the Constitution's ambiguities created a pattern of "conflict-within-consensus" that has kept the system alive for more than two centuries (Kammen 51). Mayhew would probably say Congress is working exactly as its structure encourages. That is the problem. A legislature built to help 535 politicians keep their jobs is not the same thing as a legislature built to solve national problems.

That is why reform should center on Congress. Kammen notes that Americans have treated the federal Constitution as "more nearly permanent" than their state constitutions (Kammen 51-52), so a parliamentary overhaul is not realistic. But changes aimed at legislative dysfunction are. Eliminating or limiting the filibuster would let Senate majorities govern. Stronger voting rights legislation would make it harder for states to use race-neutral rules to exclude voters, the kind of tactic Smith describes in the post-Reconstruction South (Smith 11-12). Clearer statutes would also reduce the temptation for presidents to fill gaps Congress leaves open. Olson's logic of collective action predicts that groups benefiting from the status quo will fight hard to protect it (Olson 411-412), but these reforms target the branch where the democratic breakdown is most obvious.

Congress is the branch the Constitution trusted with lawmaking, and it is the branch that fails that responsibility most clearly today. Article I created a legislature meant to connect citizens to national policy through elections, deliberation, and compromise. Mayhew shows that the modern Congress is organized around reelection instead, and Smith and Olson help explain why marginalized communities and large unorganized publics lose out when that happens. The presidency and the courts may look more active, but as Jones and Rosenberg suggest, that activity often reflects congressional weakness rather than true democratic success. I believe the government created by the Constitution can still function, but it will not be democratic enough until Congress can legislate again. The Constitution does not need to be scrapped. The first branch does.
