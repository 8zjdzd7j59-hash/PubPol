## Sample Thesis Statements (Adapt—Do Not Copy Verbatim)

Choose one direction and rewrite as **one sentence** in your own voice:

1. **Reform needed (Congress-centered):** Although the Constitution’s separation of powers still prevents concentrated tyranny, Congress’s structural design—especially equal state representation in the Senate and modern partisan gridlock—makes the system insufficiently democratic and functionally paralyzed, so major institutional reform is necessary even if the presidency and judiciary retain core constitutional roles.

2. **Mixed verdict (branch comparison):** The U.S. constitutional framework remains broadly functional because Madisonian checks and balances still constrain arbitrary power, but it is democratically deficient in representation; among the three branches, the judiciary most consistently performs its constitutional function while Congress performs worst under contemporary polarization.

3. **Targeted reform (not revolution):** The Constitution does not require replacement, but it does require major democratic reforms—particularly to Senate apportionment and the legislative process—because current structures translate minority rule into policy outcomes that disproportionately harm racial minorities and residents of high-population states.

## Key Terms to Define in Your Paper

Define these when you first use them (in your own words):

| Term | Core meaning for this paper |
|------|-----------------------------|
| **Separation of powers** | Constitutional division of legislative, executive, and judicial authority so no single actor holds all governing power (Madison, *Federalist No. 51*). |
| **Checks and balances** | Partial overlap of powers (veto, appointment, judicial review) so branches can restrain one another. |
| **Majoritarian democracy** | System where policy tends to reflect the preferences of the voting majority. |
| **Minority rights / minority rule** | Protection of dissenting groups vs. situations where a numerical minority controls institutions. |
| **Malapportionment** | Unequal voting weight across citizens (e.g., Wyoming vs. California in the Senate). |
| **Gridlock** | Legislative stalemate when divided government and veto points block policy change. |
| **Judicial review** | Courts’ power to invalidate laws/actions that violate the Constitution (*Marbury v. Madison* tradition). |
| **Federalism** | Division of authority between national and state governments (Tenth Amendment; dual sovereignty). |

## Constitutional Frameworks by Branch (Evidence Notes)

Use these as **research notes**—paraphrase and cite in your draft.

### Article I — Congress

**Design:**
- Bicameral legislature: House (population-based) + Senate (two senators per state)
- Enumerated powers; lawmaking requires both chambers + presentment to president
- Internal rules (filibuster, committee system) largely controlled by Congress itself

**Functional strengths:**
- Most directly tied to electoral accountability
- Controls budget, war declarations (formally), appointments (Senate advice and consent)
- Can check president via oversight, impeachment, appropriations

**Democratic / functional problems (develop these as claims):**
- **Equal Senate representation** violates “one person, one vote” logic applied in *Reynolds v. Sims* (1964) for state legislatures but not for the federal Senate (Article V protects this)
- **Gridlock** when parties split control or when supermajority rules block action (Levinson; Teter)
- **Policy vacuum:** when Congress stalls, other branches fill the gap—distorting intended separation of powers

**Developed argument example (model the assignment’s style):**
Congress works least well today for two reasons. First, malapportionment in the Senate means citizens in populous states exercise far less legislative influence than citizens in small states, which weakens democratic legitimacy. Second, when Congress cannot legislate, presidents expand unilateral action and courts interpret statutes in ways that effectively make policy—shifting power away from the branch most accountable to voters.

### Article II — Presidency

**Design:**
- Unitary executive; veto power; commander in chief; appointment power (with Senate); take care clause
- Electoral College selects president (not national popular vote)

**Functional strengths:**
- Can act with speed in crisis (unity and decision-making capacity)
- Veto and agenda-setting power can break or force legislative bargains
- Take Care Clause supports administrative implementation of laws

**Democratic / functional problems:**
- **Electoral College** can produce presidents who lose the national popular vote
- **Imperial / living presidency** thesis: executive power grows when Congress abdicates (Prakash; Lowi)
- Campaigns focus on swing states, reducing accountability to voters in “safe” states

**Developed argument example:**
The presidency performs better than Congress functionally but worse democratically because the same structural features that enable decisive action—the Electoral College and broad executive discretion—decouple presidential power from equal citizen influence.

### Article III — Judiciary

**Design:**
- Life tenure during good behavior; jurisdiction set by Congress; judicial review implied/developed over time
- Insulated from immediate electoral pressure

**Functional strengths:**
- Can check unconstitutional majorities and protect minority rights
- Relatively stable interpretive role when political branches conflict
- Institutional loyalty may preserve constitutional boundaries (Huq)

**Democratic / functional problems:**
- Life tenure + slow turnover can reduce responsiveness to changing public values
- Appointment politics (Senate confirmation) ties courts to partisan conflict
- Democratic legitimacy debate: unelected judges overturning popular laws

**Developed argument example:**
The judiciary works best at performing its constitutional function—interpreting law and checking abuses—precisely because it is least tied to electoral majorities, but that insulation creates democratic tension when courts decide high-salience policy questions.

## How Different Societal Groups Are Affected Differently

Weave **at least one full body paragraph** on differential impact.

| Group | Structural mechanism | Likely impact |
|-------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Racial minorities** (Black, Hispanic voters) | Concentrated in large states; Senate malapportionment | Underrepresented in Senate; less leverage over legislation and confirmations (Malhotra 2007; Griffin 2006) |
| **Urban residents** | Senate/Electoral College overweight rural states | Policy bias toward rural interests; infrastructure and spending disparities |
| **Residents of large states** (CA, TX, NY, FL) | Equal Senate seats | Reduced per-capita Senate power vs. WY, VT, AK |
| **Small-state voters** | Senate + Electoral College | Overrepresented; greater influence per voter |
| **Political minorities within states** | Federalism + rights protections + courts | May gain protection via judicial review even when outvoted locally |
| **Low-income / marginalized communities** | Gridlock slows redistributive legislation; executive agency rules fill gaps | Uneven protection depending on executive priorities and court doctrine |
| **DC / Puerto Rico residents** | Non-state status | Limited or no congressional representation despite national policy control over them |

**Developed claim to expand:**
Malapportionment is not merely abstract math—it systematically reduces the Senate influence of voters of color because those populations disproportionately live in high-population states, which means constitutional structure, not just partisan preference, shapes who wins and loses in national politics.

## Counterarguments to Address (Strengthen Your Paper)

Include at least one paragraph acknowledging the other side:

1. **Madisonian defense:** Separation of powers and federalism intentionally slow majorities to protect liberty (*Federalist No. 51*); dysfunction may be a feature, not a bug.
2. **Reform skepticism:** Constitutional conventions or major structural change could produce worse outcomes (Levinson critics; Barney Frank response in *Daedalus* forum).
3. **Congress still capable:** Despite gridlock, Congress still passes major legislation in unified government; crisis legislation (COVID relief, etc.) shows capacity.
4. **Court reform risks:** Changing the judiciary may undermine independence without fixing root causes in Congress (*Harvard Law Review* “Reform Congress, Not the Court”).

**Rebuttal strategy:** Concede what is true (checks prevent tyranny), then argue your thesis still holds (democratic deficit remains; one branch underperforms).

- Hook: recent shutdown, impeachment standoff, or 2000/2016 Electoral College/popular vote split
- Define functional vs. democratic
- Roadmap (3 parts)
- Thesis (your one sentence)

### Body Paragraph 1 — Constitutional design: what the Framers built
- Topic sentence: The Constitution creates three distinct branches with overlapping checks.
- Evidence: Articles I–III; *Federalist No. 51* (ambition counteracting ambition)
- Analysis: design prioritizes **stability and liberty** over **majoritarian speed**

### Body Paragraph 2 — Congress: democratic deficit + dysfunction
- Topic sentence: Congress is the least functional branch under current conditions.
- Evidence: Senate malapportionment; gridlock (Teter 2013; Levinson 2006)
- Analysis: explain **why** veto points + polarization = stalemate
- Link: stalemate pushes power elsewhere (transition to presidency)

### Body Paragraph 3 — Presidency and judiciary in comparison
- Topic sentence: The presidency acts decisively but expands power when Congress fails; the judiciary checks abuses but faces legitimacy costs.
- Evidence: executive unilateralism literature; judicial review role (Huq 2005; Prakash)
- Analysis: compare **functional capacity** vs. **democratic accountability**
- State which branch “works best” and define what “works” means in your thesis

### Body Paragraph 4 — Differential impact on societal groups
- Topic sentence: Structural representation problems do not affect all Americans equally.
- Evidence: Malhotra (2007); Griffin (2006); optional Lee & Oppenheimer (*Sizing Up the Senate*)
- Analysis: connect representation distortion to policy voice—not just symbolism

### Body Paragraph 5 — Reform: major or targeted?
- Topic sentence: Your reform judgment (major vs. targeted) follows from prior analysis.
- Evidence: Levinson reform proposals; Article V difficulty; alternative reforms (filibuster change, proportional Senate adjustment proposals, Electoral College reform debates)
- Analysis: explain **feasibility** and **normative justification**
- Address counterargument briefly

### Conclusion (1 paragraph)
- Restate thesis (new words)
- Summarize branch comparison + group impact + reform claim
- Broader implication: what happens to democratic legitimacy if structures stay unchanged?
